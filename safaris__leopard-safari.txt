Follow alarm calls, pugmarks and the warmth of sun-struck granite with a master tracker, as resident leopards surface on the rocks at first and last light.
The anatomy of a tracking drive
Long before sunrise, your Captain is already listening. In Jawai the leopards do not hide in dense forest. They rest on and inside the great granite domes, and the land itself tells you where they are. A langur barking from a ridge, a peacock's sharp alarm, fresh pugmarks in a sandy riverbed: each sign is read and weighed.
As the sun warms the rock, leopards often emerge onto ledges and cave mouths to bask. We position the vehicle quietly, switch off the engine and simply wait. Patience is the whole art.
Ethical distance standards
We never block an animal's path, never crowd a sighting and never use calls or bait. Engines are switched off during observation and we leave before the animal is disturbed. A leopard sighting is never guaranteed (any operator who promises one is misleading you), but the open granite terrain gives Jawai one of the highest daytime observation probabilities anywhere.
What to bring
Neutral clothing: khaki, olive, stone. Warm layers for winter mornings (it can be 8–12°C at dawn).
A 100–400mm or 70–200mm lens for photographers; we carry beanbags.
Hat, sunscreen, and a scarf for dusty tracks.
